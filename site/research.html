Research

Stars are hotter and denser than any lab on Earth.

That makes stellar cores and core-collapse supernovae laboratories for feebly interacting particles: the QCD axion, axion-like particles (ALPs) and their relatives, all well-motivated dark-matter candidates. Made deep inside a star, they can reveal themselves as X-ray and gamma-ray signals, by decaying or by converting into photons in magnetic fields.

Comparing those signals with what telescopes see tells us which models survive.

PhD thesis · 2024–2027

Stars and supernovae as axion laboratories: searches for feebly interacting particles with X- and gamma-ray telescopes

The full chain, from star to signal

  1. 01

    Production

    Hot, dense plasma makes axions through photon–nucleus collisions, nucleon bremsstrahlung and nuclear transitions.

    My part: rates · MESA stellar models

  2. 02

    Escape

    Feebly interacting, they leave the star almost unimpeded, carrying energy out but no light.

    My part: emission spectra and fluxes

  3. 03

    Conversion or decay

    They turn back into photons in the magnetic field of the progenitor star, the Galaxy or a galaxy cluster, or by decaying in flight.

    My part: conversion and decay probabilities

  4. 04

    Detection

    The signal lands in X-ray and gamma-ray telescopes: NuSTAR, Fermi-LAT, eROSITA, XMM-Newton, Swift, Chandra and COMPTEL.

    My part: data reduction · likelihood analysis

Three ways to catch an axion

i · Hot, dense matter

Production in stars and supernovae

  • Type Ic supernovae probe decaying ALPs beyond SN 1987A [2]
  • The next Galactic Type Ib/c supernova could detect the QCD axion down to mₐ = 10⁻⁴ eV [1]
  • The Sun as a natural helioscope with NuSTAR [6, 7]
  • Heavy ALPs (30 to 500 keV) from the massive stars of M82 [5]
  • The axion–nucleon coupling from ⁵⁷Fe in Betelgeuse [4]

ii · Cosmic-scale converters

Diffuse fluxes and cluster magnets

  • The diffuse ALP background from every supernova in cosmic history, and its Fermi-LAT sensitivity [3]
  • Galaxy clusters as megaparsec-scale magnets: high-frequency gravitational waves turning into photons in the Virgo Cluster, seen with eROSITA and COMPTEL, one to two orders of magnitude beyond Milky Way bounds (in review)

iii · Instruments

X-ray optics for IAXO

  • The NuSTAR glass-optics inventory and optics simulations for IAXO’s X-ray telescopes [8]
  • NuSTAR-type optics measured at the SOLEIL synchrotron, an input to BabyIAXO’s ray-tracing model [11]
  • The veto system and pressure settings of the IAXO Micromegas detector [12]
  • Contributions to the IAXO physics case [10]

What’s next

A

A thermometer for dying stars

In the last burning stages of a massive star the core heats from about 10 to 300 keV, thermally exciting nuclear transitions that emit axions. Each transition switches on at a different temperature, so the resulting X-ray line spectrum would act as a thermometer of the core and could flag a supergiant approaching collapse, much like pre-supernova neutrinos.

B

Magnetised plasmas, from stars to galaxies

The Wolf–Rayet star HD 45166 carries a 43 kG surface field, compact enough to convert much heavier axions than the Galactic field can, inside a dense, wind-fed magnetosphere that can resonantly boost the signal. NuSTAR, XMM-Newton and Swift data plus MESA models would give the first axion bounds from a Wolf–Rayet star. On galactic scales: the turbulent core of M82.

C

Diffuse backgrounds of new physics

If feebly interacting particles exist, every star and supernova in cosmic history has left a diffuse background of them. After the Virgo Cluster, the next target is the Coma Cluster, with archival XMM-Newton and NuSTAR data, for axions and for high-frequency gravitational waves alike.

Stripped-envelope supernovae for QCD axion detection

F. R. Candón, D. F. G. Fiorillo, Á. Gil Muyor, H.-T. Janka, G. G. Raffelt, E. Vitagliano

In plain words · Stars that lose their outer layers before exploding carry the strongest surface magnetic fields of any supernova progenitor, ideal for turning axions into gamma rays. The next such supernova in our Galaxy could reveal the QCD axion for masses down to 10⁻⁴ eV.

Probing the axion–nucleon coupling with supergiant stars

F. R. Candón, P. Casaseca, M. Giannotti, M. Kaltschmidt, J. Ruz, J. K. Vogel

In plain words · In the hot core of a supergiant like Betelgeuse, iron-57 nuclei are thermally excited; as they relax they can emit axions, which the Galaxy’s magnetic field turns into a sharp X-ray line. NuSTAR doesn’t see it, which limits how strongly axions couple to protons and neutrons.

My part · Built the MESA stellar models; computed axion production and conversion in the Galactic magnetic field; ran the NuSTAR data and likelihood analysis.
